Appendix · Sources and method

The data in this simulator were processed from the evidence and the model presented in the book The Last Forecast by Enrico Lorenzo De Vito, and from its software package (aimodel v3.1). The book provides the logic: how exposure turns into substitution, how fast adoption spreads, how many new tasks appear and when. Published occupation-level studies provide the starting exposure of each job. The simulator is a visual simplification of the book's model, not a separate forecast.

Logic taken from the book

  • Adoption curve (appendix A1): an S-curve that starts from zero in 2026. Central year 2035 for advanced economies (range 2030–2040), slope 0.425 a year (0.25–0.60).
  • Regional lags: emerging economies 3 years later (1–5), low-income countries 6.5 years later (3–10), with reference to Comin and Hobijn (2010).
  • Substitution: 55% of exposed work is substitutable (40–70%, IMF SDN/2024/001), and 47.5% of it is automated by 2046 (25–70%, Eloundou et al. 2024; McKinsey Global Institute 2023).
  • New tasks: 0.55 new tasks for each task lost (0.25–0.85, Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019), arriving 7 years later (4–10, Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson 2021; Autor et al. 2024).
  • Exposure growth: exposure grows by 30% by 2046 (up to 60%) and never exceeds 95%. The book links this growth to wider capabilities and to the robot stock growing 9% a year (IFR 2025).
  • Scenarios (employment module, config/employment.json), at the centre of each range: Reference: 0.55 new tasks per task lost after 7 years, exposure +30%. Effective compensation: 1.025 new tasks after 4 years. Slow retraining: 0.45 new tasks after 9 years. Persistent displacement: 0.175 new tasks after 12 years, 67.5% automated by 2046, exposure +60%. The book assigns no probability to any of them.
  • Comparison: every number is the difference from a world without AI, as in the book's paired simulations.
  • Reference result: in advanced economies, extra unemployment from AI has a median of +4.3 points in 2036 and +1.4 points in 2046 (4,000 draws).

Data by occupation

  • AI exposure: share of each occupation's tasks where a GPT-type system can cut the time needed by at least half (beta measure: fully exposed tasks plus half of the tasks exposed with extra software). Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin and Rock, "GPTs are GPTs: Labor market impact potential of LLMs", Science 384 (2024); occupation-level data in the authors' public repository github.com/openai/GPTs-are-GPTs (file occ_level.csv). The paper gives two ratings for the same tasks, by human experts and by GPT-4; both can be selected. Human ratings are the default. For frontier occupations the gamma measure is used (all tasks exposed with extra software), from the same file.
  • Robot exposure: probability of computerisation of each occupation, Frey and Osborne, "The Future of Employment" (Oxford Martin School 2013; Technological Forecasting and Social Change 114, 2017). It covers both software and machines, so it is used only for manual and physical occupations (food preparation, sales floor, construction, production, transport) and set to zero for office and professional occupations, where the AI rating applies.
  • Frontier occupations: in the Stanford Digital Economy Lab / ADP "Canaries" data used in the book (vintage September 2026), employment of 22–25 year olds fell by 4.5% for software developers and by 7.5% for customer service between August 2025 and August 2026, and the decline reached ages 26–34. Only the last year is used. These two occupations adopt from 2030, the earliest central year in the book's range, instead of the scenario value. This is a choice of the simulator, not a book parameter.
  • Why frontier occupations get different values: the book's substitution (55%) and automation (47.5%) are central values for the whole economy, and the book does not assign them to single occupations. For the two frontier occupations the simulator uses three different choices, all declared: exposure with software tools (gamma measure of the same study: tasks exposed once tools such as coding agents are built on the model; software developers 84% by human experts, 95% by GPT-4, against 45% and 87% with the beta measure); substitution at 70% and automation by 2046 at 70% at least, the top of the book's ranges. These are assumptions for jobs where the technology is already in use, not estimates for each occupation.
  • Observed pace: for the two frontier occupations in advanced economies, the end of a run also shows what the last 12 months of the Stanford/ADP data would imply if the same yearly pace held over the selected period. August 2025 – August 2026, all ages: software developers −0.4% to −2.1%, customer service −1.2% to −3.1% (the files give an index for each age group but not its size, so the total is shown between a young-heavy and an older-heavy age mix); ages 22–25: −4.5% and −7.5%. Only the last year is used because the AI boom in these jobs shows up from 2025, while 2022–2024 also reflect post-pandemic over-hiring and higher interest rates. US data, shown as a comparison with the scenarios, not as a forecast.
  • Author's forecast (beyond the book): the default scenario for every occupation, declared as a forecast and not as a book estimate. It follows chapter 36. Exposure uses the gamma measure of Eloundou et al. (tasks exposed once software tools are built on the model) for every occupation, with no further widening, since that measure already includes the tools. For routine office jobs (office clerk, accountant, customer service) it takes the higher of that value and the Frey and Osborne probability of computerisation (0.96, 0.94 and 0.55, appendix of the 2013 paper), so that classic office automation is counted too; never above 95%. Substitution and automation by 2046 depend on the type of occupation, following the book's distinction between exposure and complementarity (IMF SDN/2024/001): routine office and creative work 85% and 85%; the architect role 70% and 70%; professions with responsibility and human contact (doctor, nurse, teacher, lawyer) 55% and 47.5%, the book's central values; manual jobs 55% and 70%, reached mainly through robots. New tasks inside the same occupation: 0.15 for each task lost, within 3 years. The central adoption year is calibrated on the decline observed in the last 12 months for software developers and customer service, and for office clerks on the customer service series, the closest office job with observed data; the other occupations use 2033, close to those calibrated years. With these values the largest possible loss by 2046 is about 58%: 95% exposure, 85% of it substitutable and 85% of that automated, minus the new roles. Results are rounded to whole percentages: differences of a few points between occupations are not meaningful.
  • New tasks inside the occupation: in the book, new tasks appear anywhere in the economy. In the book scenarios the simulator counts them inside the same occupation, which overstates what is left of that occupation; the author's forecast keeps only the new roles that stay in the occupation.
  • Range: at the end of a run the simulator also shows the result of the same job, region and period under the four book scenarios and both exposure ratings, from the lowest to the highest. As in the book, the range matters more than the single path.
  • Exposure is not job loss: as the book stresses, both studies measure technical possibility. The book's logic decides how much of it becomes substitution, and when.

Other choices made for this simulator

  • Countries and continents: the book uses three groups (advanced, emerging, low-income) and notes that this geography is too coarse. Each country or continent here takes the timing of its group: USA, Canada, Japan, Europe and Oceania as advanced; China, India, Russia, the rest of Asia and the Americas as emerging; Africa as low-income.
  • Robots as a separate wave: in the book, robotics only widens AI exposure. Here robots can be shown as a second wave, 5 years after AI, using the same formula. Turn it off to stay closer to the book.
  • Central values: the simulator runs one path with the centre of each scenario, while the book reports the spread of 4,000 draws. Use the scenarios and the sliders to explore the ranges.
  • Not modelled here: wages, demand for the cheaper product, entry barriers for young people and the other mechanisms of the book's full model. New tasks are counted inside the same occupation, while in the book they can appear anywhere in the economy.

Like the book, the simulator does not forecast what will happen to a single job. It shows what the model's assumptions imply.

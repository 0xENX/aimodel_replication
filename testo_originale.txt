AI, Robotics and the GlobalEconomy 2026-
2046: An Independent Critical Review
Executive summary
Verdict: the paper asks the right questions but points at the wrong main risk. The
evidence through 2026 shows no aggregate employment collapse; it shows a falling labour
share, a sharp drop in hiring of young workers in AI-exposed jobs, and public finances
already near 100% debt-to-GDP before anyAI shock. Distribution and fiscal capacity are
the dominant mechanisms. Unemployment is not a collapse, but once labour demand is
derived from technology rather than tied to labour supply, it becomes a serious and
persistent risk: in most simulations it is still higher in 2046 than without AI.
What the evidence supports:
Exposure is broad, impact is narrow so far. About 40% of global employment is
exposed to AI (IMF), but only 3.3% sits in the ILO's highest-exposure tier. Exposure
measures technical feasibility, not job loss.
The first measurable effect is on entry-level hiring. Employment of 22-25 year olds in
AI-exposed occupations is 19% below the trend of less-exposed peers, mostly through
fewer hires, not layoffs (Stanford, Aug 2026).
Macro productivity gains are uncertain by an order of magnitude. Credible estimates
range from under 0.7% total-factor-productivity over ten years (Acemoglu) to far
larger figures; the paper rightly refuses to pick one.
The fiscal exposure is specific. In the OECD, social contributions (25.5%) and
personal income tax (23.7%) are the two largest revenue sources, and both lean on
wages.
Where I disagree with the paper:
1. It omits demographics as a counterforce. Labour supply is slowing and populations
are ageing; in many economies automation will fill gaps rather than create surplus
labour. This changes the sign of the problem by country.
2. Its "profits eventually constrained by weak demand" loop is not supported. In the
calibrated model of the technical annex, demand falls short of supply by more than
0.1% of output in only 31% of 4,000 simulations, the largest shortfall is 2.5% of output,
and the two demand parameters explain at most 1.5% of the variance of any outcome.
Concentration of gains, not a profit collapse, is the robust outcome.
3. It underweights the timing channel. Displacement arrives faster than new tasks:
unemployment in advanced economies rises by a median 4.3 points by 2036, and half
of the simulations peak between 2036 and 2041. In 2046 it is still 1.4 points higher in the

A no-AI counterfactual sharpens the point. In 2036 world output is 1.3% higher than
without AI, but the bottom 50% of the world's population has 1.0% less purchasing power,
and is worse off in 92% of simulations. About 84% of the world's population lives in
income groups that are worse off than without AI in 2036, almost all of them by less than
1% at the median; the losses are much larger for workers displaced before new work
appears. By 2046 the bottom half is still worse off in 89% of simulations, by a median
3.3%. Distribution policy decides whetherAI ends up making the majority better off or
worse off: a package built around an AI dividend turns the bottom half's 2036 outcome
into a 3.0% gain.
All quantitative results in this report come from the calibrated model documented in the
Technical Annex at the end (4,000 paired simulations with and without AI, calibrated to
IMF, ILO, OECD, UN and World Inequality Database data and validated against historical
trends). They are conditional simulations, not forecasts. An earlier version of this report
used a simpler illustrative model whose effects were roughly ten times larger; section 14
of the annex explains why the numbers changed. This edition widens the range of wage
rigidity to match recent evidence, corrects the wage equation and derives labour demand
from production and technology instead of letting it follow labour supply; section 14.4 of
the annex documents what that changed: a larger and much more persistent rise in
unemployment and a larger loss for the bottom half.
Critique ofthe original paper
The paper is careful about causality but leaves its key mechanisms untested and omits
three variables that change the conclusions: demographics, timing, and the geography of
where profits are booked.

Timing and adjustment speed. Harm comes from the gap between displacement and
new task creation, not from the end state. The paper treats outcomes as levels, not
paths.
Where profits are booked. AI gains concentrate in a few firms and jurisdictions. A
country can host the users ofAI while the taxable rents are booked elsewhere. This
matters more for most governments than the global total.
Prices. Cheaper services raise real incomes even with flat nominal wages. The paper
names this channel but does not weigh it.
Investment as demand. AI capital spending is itself a demand source; the IMF
estimates AI-related investment added about 0.5 pp to US GDP growth in 2025.
Methodological problems:
The six "primary indicators" mix levels (GDP), ratios (profitability) and composites
(instability) without saying how they interact. Drawing them on one normalised chart
invites the reader to compare incomparable scales.
"Violence and Social Instability" as one indicator merges protest, crime and political
violence, which the paper itself says are distinct.
The paper has no country dimension. Exposure in low-income countries is about a third
of advanced-economy levels; a global average hides opposite stories.
Current evidence
The evidence shows wide potential exposure, modest measured macro effects, a
concentrated hit to young workers, and a long-running shift of income from labour to
capital that predates generative AI.

How to read this evidence:
Exposure figures are ceilings. Both the IMF and ILO stress that exposure means a task
could be done byAI, not that it will be, or that the job disappears.
The youth effect is descriptive, not causal. The Stanford authors say so explicitly, and
total employment in their sample still grew. It is the most important early signal
because it works through hiring, which aggregate unemployment data catch late.
Micro gains exceed macro gains. A 14% gain in one function becomes a fraction of a
percent of GDP once weighted by the share of the economy affected. This gap
between task-level and economy-level effects is the main reason forecasts diverge.
The labour share was falling before ChatGPT. Automation, market concentration and
the pandemic all contributed. AI amplifies a trend; it does not start one.
WEF figures are employer surveys, useful for direction, weak as forecasts.
Reconstructed model
The outcome turns on one variable the paper treats as a consequence: household real
income after transfers. Automation affects it through three channels with opposite
signs: lower labour demand, new tasks that reinstate work, and lower prices. Public
finance feeds back into it through transfers.

Read clockwise from the household box: income drives consumption, consumption drives
output and profits, output feeds tax revenue, and revenue funds transfers back to
households. Two inputs sit outside the loop and decide its sign: how fast new tasks
appear relative to automation, and how fast labour supply shrinks with ageing. Not drawn,
to keep the picture legible: labour income also feeds tax revenue directly, and AI
investment raises output independently of consumption.
The simulations in this report implement this loop in a calibrated model of the world
economy, documented in full in the Technical Annex (section 4 and Appendix A). In brief:
Three regions and three income groups. Advanced, emerging and low-income
economies, each with a bottom 50%, a middle 40% and a top 10%, calibrated to 2025-
2026 data.
Tasks, not jobs. AI automates a share of tasks and new human tasks appear with a lag,
following Acemoglu and Restrepo. Productivity gains followAcemoglu's (2025)
method.
Complete accounting. Every flow of income between households, firms, governments
and regions is tracked, and the accounts balance in every year of every simulation.
Uncertainty made explicit. Twenty-one uncertain parameters are drawn from ranges
in the literature. Each of 4,000 draws is simulated twice, with and without AI, so the
difference isolates AI's effect.
Labour market. Employment cannot exceed the available labour force; when
employers want more workers than exist, wages rise instead. When there is slack,
wages fall only slowly, at rates taken from recent wage Phillips-curve estimates, so
displacement shows up as unemployment for years. Labour demand is derived from
reconstructed causal model · 12 nodes, one demand-and-fiscal loop

Sensitivity test on the paper's key claim. The paper argues that weak demand may
eventually constrain profits. A global sensitivity analysis over 23,552 model runs (annex,
section 9) shows that the response of investment to profits and the pass-through of weak
demand to output each explain at most 1.5% of the variance of any outcome. What
matters is the timing of adoption, how fast new human tasks appear and, for
unemployment, how rigid wages are. A profit squeeze is not impossible in principle, but
nothing within the calibrated parameter ranges produces one.
Gap between labour supply and labour demand. The gap shows up as unemployment,
and it is much larger and longer than the earlier edition of this report found, because
labour demand now follows technology and output and wages may be as rigid as recent
evidence suggests. In advanced economies the median increase is 4.3 points in 2036 (5th￾95th percentile 1.4 to 10.7), roughly 26 million people, and 1.9 points in emerging
economies, roughly 47 million. The median path peaks at about 4.9 points in 2039 and is
still 1.4 points higher in 2046. In the fifth of simulations where wages respond least,
unemployment is still about 4 points higher in 2046. In reality part of this would appear as
lower participation, shorter hours and underemployment rather than open
unemployment.

Labour demand recovers only partly, and income shares do not. In advanced economies
labour demand is furthest below the no-AI path in 2039, 4.7 points below it and 9.6 points
below labour supply. By 2046 it has regained most of that gap but is still 1.3 points below,
and in six simulations out of ten unemployment is still more than half a point higher. In
emerging and low-income economies, where adoption arrives later, the gap is barely
closing in 2046 (+2.3 and +2.2 points). Lower wages bring back only part of the jobs: by
2046 wages in advanced economies are 9% below the no-AI path in the median simulation
and 25% below in the worst 5%. Because the wage bill falls with employment,
unemployment lowers labour income instead of only spreading it differently.
Why labour demand still partly returns, and what the model leaves out. Demand
recovers because new tasks appear with a lag and lower wages make some human work
worth doing again; it does not track labour supply. Labour supply still follows
demographics and does not react to AI, and there is no mismatch between the skills of
displaced workers and the new tasks. In reality young people can stay longer in education,
leave the labour force or emigrate, and displaced workers may not fit the new tasks. With
mismatch the gap would close more slowly; with falling participation part of the
unemployment would become hidden. The model does not include either (annex, section
15).

Fiscal sustainability
The gap is fundable on paper but not funded under current tax systems: keeping public
finances whole would require taxing the extra capital income at an effective rate of
about 41%, roughly double the 19% at which capital income is taxed in advanced
economies today. Capital is taxed more lightly than labour, it is mobile, and AI rents
concentrate in a few firms and jurisdictions.
The table shows advanced economies, where the fiscal effect is largest, for the median
simulation of each scenario. It is a conditional result of the calibrated model, not a
forecast.
Assumptions: tax rates calibrated to IMF and OECD revenue data, with labour income
taxed at an effective 28% and capital income at about 19% in advanced economies.
Pensions rise with ageing in both worlds, so even without AI advanced-economy debt
rises from 108% to about 132% of GDP by 2046 (annex, section 10). In 2036 AI lowers
labour-tax revenue by 1.26% of GDP and raises capital-tax revenue by 0.84%; higher
unemployment benefits and smaller items make up the rest.
What the numbers say:
The tax base shifts more than it shrinks. GDP keeps growing in all three scenarios;
income moves from wages to profits. A tax system that follows the income can stay
whole.
Current systems do not follow it. OECD revenue leans on wages, and the share of
personal income tax coming from employed labour already fell in two thirds of OECD
countries between 2011 and 2023. The IMF notes that capital income is taxed more
lightly than labour and recommends strengthening general capital income taxes rather
than a special tax on AI or robots.

The cost is moderate but persistent, and the tail matters. In polarisation the primary
balance worsens by about 0.4% of GDP and debt is 6.5 points higher by 2046; in
disruption the figures are 1.2% of GDP and 13 points, about half the effect of ageing. It
arrives when fiscal space is already thin, and in the worst 5% of simulations debt is 26
points higher.
Distribution across countries is the hardest problem. A country that uses AI services
but does not host the firms earning the rents loses the wage base without gaining the
profit base. The global minimum corporate tax helps at the margin but does not
reallocate rents to user countries.
Compensating mechanisms that could plausibly fund obligations:
1. Broader capital income taxation (dividends, capital gains at realisation or death,
closing preferential regimes): matches the shift in the base directly; limited by mobility
and political resistance.
2. Consumption taxes: robust to the labour/capital split, but regressive unless paired
with transfers.
3. Rent and land taxation: captures gains capitalised into property and scarce assets;
slow to implement.
4. Public or broad-based ownership stakes (sovereign funds, employee ownership):
turns capital income into household income without relying on tax rates; requires
upfront capital.
5. Reconsidering accelerated depreciation for labour-replacing investment: the IMF
suggests incentives that speed displacement should be reviewed.
A "robot tax" is the weakest option: it is hard to define the base, it slows adoption of
productivity gains, and it raises little revenue.
Social stability and automated policing
Economic stress raises the odds of unrest, but institutions decide whether it turns into
instability; and automated policing is limited far more by law and error rates than by
budgets. The paper's chain from job loss to unrest to surveillance is possible, but each link
is conditional.

The real risk is asymmetric drift. If welfare provision weakens while surveillance
capacity grows, the relationship between citizen and state changes without any single
decision. This is a governance risk, highest where courts, press and data-protection
authorities are weak.
Accountability is the binding constraint. Automated assistance with a named human
decision-maker is manageable. Autonomous coercive decisions break existing liability
and due-process rules and should be treated as a bright line, not a budget question.
Measurement. The paper's single "violence and social instability" indicator should be
split: peaceful protest, civil unrest, crime and political violence have different causes
and opposite policy implications. Protest is often a sign of a working democracy, not of
breakdown.
Scenarios and plausibility
Under current policies, structural disruption (C) occurs in 74% of simulations,
polarisation (B) in 23% and inclusive growth (A) in 8%. I keep the paper's three
scenarios and add two it omits, demographic substitution (D) and stalled adoption (E).
The model treats these two as conditions within the main scenarios rather than as
separate outcomes.
The shares come from the model, not from judgement: each of the 4,000 simulations is
classified by what happens to households in 2036 (definitions in the reconstructed-model
section). They are probabilities conditional on the model and its parameter ranges, not
forecasts of the world. If the ranges are wrong, so are the shares; the technical annex
shows which parameters matter most. They replace the 0-100 plausibility scores of the
earlier version, which were a judgement.

than to labour supply, unemployment alone pushes 46% of all simulations past the 3-point

three simulations in four fall in C. The share is sensitive to the threshold: with a 4-point

further ifAI capabilities grew faster than the model's exposure ranges allow, a case the
model excludes (annex, section 15).
WhyA is rare under current policies. A needs larger productivity gains than the centre of
the evidence and a larger share ofAI rents reaching workers, and even then
unemployment rises. Markets do not guarantee either; policy can help with both. Under

Mechanisms that may dominate the paper's own:
1. The apprenticeship break. If firms stop hiring juniors because AI does junior tasks, the
pipeline that produces experienced workers thins out. The cost appears 10-15 years
later as a shortage of senior skills. This is underrated everywhere.
2. Demographic substitution. In ageing economies, AI and robotics may be the main way
to maintain output and care services with fewer workers. Here the policy risk is
adopting too slowly, not too fast.
3. Price deflation in services. IfAI makes legal, medical, educational and administrative
services much cheaper, real purchasing power can rise even with flat wages. This is a
potentially large positive pathway, but it is unmeasured; unmeasured negative
pathways (worse job quality, workplace surveillance, market concentration, energy
costs) deserve the same weight.
4. Geographic concentration of rents. Who owns frontier models and compute will
shape national tax bases more than global GDP does.
What would improve outcomes most, in order of expected impact: (1) tax systems that
follow income from wages to capital, coordinated internationally; (2) income support
decoupled from employment history, with active retraining; (3) protecting entry-level
hiring and training pathways; (4) wider ownership of productive capital; (5) bright-line
legal limits on autonomous coercive AI.

Overall verdictfor 2036
On balance, across all simulations most of the world's population is slightly worse off in
2036 than it would be without AI, and unemployment is markedly higher. About 84% of
people live in income groups whose purchasing power is lower than without AI, almost all
by less than 1% at the median; labour demand is about 2.1% lower worldwide for the same
labour supply, and unemployment in advanced economies is 4.3 points higher. These are
group averages: the loss is concentrated on workers displaced before new work appears
and on young people entering exposed occupations, for whom it is much larger. The gains
go mainly to owners of capital, especially in advanced economies, and to workers in
scarce physical and care occupations. Cheaper services and better access to knowledge
are real benefits; if they are worth what the model's plausible range assumes, the bottom
half is still slightly worse off on average (−0.1%) and worse off in 55% of simulations.
The data do not support the claim that AI makes everyone worse off, nor that it causes a
general crisis by 2036. Capital owners and some occupations gain, and in scenario A the
bottom half is better off. Nor do they support the claim that the damage is temporary:
unemployment stays above the no-AI path for most of the horizon, and in 89% of
simulations the bottom half is still behind the no-AI world in 2046. The objective summary
is: slightly worse for the majority during the transition, much worse for a displaced
minority, better for owners of capital, and not worse by necessity.
The decisive point is that this outcome is not fixed by the technology. The gap between
scenarios A and C in 2036, 1.7 points of purchasing power for the bottom half, is larger
than the average effect ofAI itself (1.0), and the gap in unemployment between flexible
and rigid wages is larger still. Whether 2036 feels like progress or like a lost decade will
depend less on what AI can do than on whether tax systems follow income from wages to
capital, whether entry-level careers are protected, and whether income support is
decoupled from employment before the transition peaks, not after.

Early-warning triggers
The measures above should not wait for a crisis to be visible in unemployment data, which
lag by years. Governments should monitor three indicators and expand support
automatically when two of them are crossed:
1. The youth hiring gap spreads beyond age 30, or exceeds 25% in AI-exposed
occupations.
2. The labour share falls faster than 0.5 percentage points a year for two consecutive
years.
3. Labour demand (vacancies and hires) in high-exposure occupations falls for two
consecutive years while GDP grows.
Timeline
2026-2028: prepare. Agree international rules on taxing AI rents; launch first-job and
retraining programmes; set up the monitoring triggers.
2029-2032: build capacity. Make income support independent of employment;
capitalise AI dividend funds; scale investment in care, health and education.
2033-2036: absorb the peak. Let automatic triggers expand support as displacement
peaks; protect pensions; keep the pipeline of junior workers open.

Honestlimits
These results come from a calibrated but simplified model. They show the direction
and relative strength of each lever, not precise outcomes; section 15 of the technical
annex lists the model's limitations.
The cost is real. Unfinanced, the four-measure package worsens the world primary
balance by about 0.6% of GDP a year and adds about 7 points to world public debt by
2046. Financing it means taxing or sharing income that is concentrated among firms
and countries with strong bargaining power.
The hardest obstacle is political, not technical. Without international coordination,
countries that act alone risk losing investment to those that do not.
Timing decides everything. The same package introduced after the unemployment
peak, which in half of the simulations falls between 2036 and 2041, arrives when the
damage is already done. The window for prevention is roughly the next ten years.

that combines the task-based framework ofAcemoglu and Restrepo with stock-flow

governments. We calibrate it to IMF, ILO, OECD, UN and World Inequality Database data,
validate it against historical labour-share trends and IMF fiscal projections, and propagate
parameter uncertainty through 4,000 paired Monte Carlo simulations with and without
the AI wave.
In 2036 world GDP is a median 1.3% higher than without AI (5th-95th percentile: 0.3% to
3.5%). The labour share is 3.0 percentage points lower (0.75 to 7.6). Real consumption per
head of the bottom 50% is 1.0% lower (−4.9% to +0.1%), and lower in 92% of simulations;
by 2046 the loss reaches 3.3%. The top 10% gains 3.1% (0.7% to 7.8%) in every simulation.
Unemployment in advanced economies is a median 4.3 points above the no-AI path in
2036 (1.4 to 10.7); the median of each simulation's own peak is 6.3 points (2.6 to 12.8), and
in six simulations out of ten unemployment is still more than half a point higher in 2046.
The paper's hypothesised demand collapse does not emerge in any plausible part of the
parameter space; the dominant mechanisms are a redistribution from labour to capital
and from AI-using to AI-owning countries, and a long period of higher unemployment,
because lower wages bring back only part of the work that automation removes.

of automation, on how rigid wages are, on how fast new human tasks appear and on how
far lower wages restore labour demand; demand behaviour matters little. Among nine
costed interventions, none protects the bottom half by itself in virtually every simulation:
an AI dividend comes closest (90% of simulations in 2036) but fades by 2046 because it
brings back no jobs. A package of an AI dividend, income support decoupled from
employment, retraining and profit-sharing turns AI's effect on the bottom 50% into a

1.Introduction
The question is not whetherAI raises output, which almost every estimate agrees it
does, but whether the income it creates reaches households and governments in time
to sustain demand, public services and political legitimacy. This paper answers that
question quantitatively for 2026-2046, with explicit uncertainty.
The starting point is a hypothesis paper that proposed a causal chain: automation
displaces labour, displaced workers consume less, weak demand eventually constrains
profits, labour-based tax bases erode while welfare needs rise, and social stability and the
relation between citizens and the state deteriorate. The paper deliberately stopped short
of quantifying any link and asked for an independent reconstruction. This paper provides
it.

We make four contributions.
1. A model that connects the links. We combine the task-based framework of
automation (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2018, 2019), which is standard in the labour
literature, with stock-flow consistent accounting (Godley and Lavoie 2007), which
tracks every flow of income between households, firms, governments and regions. The
combination lets the demand and fiscal feedbacks that the hypothesis paper
describes either appear or fail to appear, rather than being assumed.
2. Calibration from data, with uncertainty propagated. Every parameter is either taken
from a cited source or given a range; 4,000 Monte Carlo draws, each run with and
without the AI wave, produce probability statements conditional on those ranges.
3. A test of which assumptions matter. Variance-based global sensitivity analysis (Sobol
2001; Saltelli et al. 2008) ranks the parameters by how much of the uncertainty in each
outcome they explain.
4. Costed policy evaluation. Nine interventions are simulated on the same draws, with
their fiscal cost and their effect on the bottom half of the income distribution.
The main findings are these. AI raises output modestly in the first decade, by a median
1.3% by 2036, consistent with conservative published estimates. It lowers the labour
share by about 3 points by 2036 and 6 points by 2046, and the losses fall on the bottom
half while gains accrue to the top tenth. Unemployment in advanced economies rises by a
median 4.3 points by 2036, peaks around 2039 and is still 1.4 points higher in 2046,
because labour demand follows technology and lower wages restore only part of it. The
demand collapse proposed by the hypothesis paper does not appear, because the shift in
income is gradual and investment absorbs part of the higher profits. The fiscal gap is real
and grows with unemployment; it is manageable only if tax systems follow income from
wages to capital. The outcome is decided mainly by timing, by the extent of automation
and by how the labour market absorbs displacement, all of which policy can influence.
Section 2 reviews the literature. Section 3 summarises and critiques the hypothesis paper.
Sections 4 to 6 present the model, its calibration and its validation. Sections 7 to 10 report
results, sensitivity and public finance. Sections 11 to 13 cover social stability, policy and a
snapshot of 2036. Section 14 documents how this paper differs from its earlier illustrative
version, and section 15 sets out limitations and a proposed empirical study.

most for this paper. The review is organised by mechanism. Searches covered NBER, IMF,
ILO, OECD, BIS and peer-reviewed journals through October 2026; values cited were
checked against the source or its official working-paper version.

2.1Task-based models of automation
Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018, 2019) model production as a set of tasks performed by
labour or capital. Automation moves tasks from labour to capital (the displacement
effect), while new tasks in which labour has a comparative advantage reinstate it (the
reinstatement effect). Their decomposition of US data finds that in 1947-1987
displacement (0.48% a year) and reinstatement (0.47% a year) roughly offset each other,
while in 1987-2017 displacement accelerated to 0.70% a year and reinstatement slowed to
0.35%, lowering the task content of labour by about 10%. In manufacturing displacement
reached about 1.1% a year. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2022) attribute 50-70% of the
changes in the US wage structure over four decades to task displacement.
Acemoglu (2025) applies this framework to AI: the total-factor-productivity gain equals
the share of GDP in tasks affected multiplied by the average cost saving. With existing
exposure and productivity estimates he obtains no more than a 0.66% TFP gain over ten
years, and finds that AI is likely to widen the gap between capital and labour income.
Korinek and Suh (2024) show that the outcome is a race between automation and capital
accumulation: slow automation can raise wages indefinitely, while fast automation can
lower them before full automation is reached.
2.2 Diffusion and the productivity lag
General-purpose technologies take decades to show up in productivity. Factory
electrification reached about half of mechanical drive only four decades after the first
central power stations (David 1990). Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson (2021) explain the
lag as a "productivity J-curve": intangible investments are not measured early, so
measured productivity first understates and later overstates the true gain. Comin and
Hobijn (2010) find an average adoption lag of 45 years across 166 countries, shorter for
newer technologies.
2.3Evidence on AI and labour markets
Exposure is broad. About 40% of global employment is exposed to AI, 60% in advanced
economies and 26% in low-income countries; in advanced economies 33 of those 60
points are in jobs with low complementarity (Cazzaniga et al. 2024). The ILO's refined
index finds one worker in four in an occupation with some generative-AI exposure and
3.3% in the highest tier (Gmyrek et al. 2025). Eloundou et al. (2024) estimate that about
1.8% of US jobs have more than half of their tasks exposed to large language models alone,
rising to about 46% once complementary software is considered.
Measured effects are narrower so far. A generative-AI assistant raised customer-support
productivity by 14% on average and 34% for novices (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond
2025). Employment of 22-25 year olds in the most exposed occupations stands 19% below

2.4 Robots
One more industrial robot per thousand workers reduced the US employment-to￾population ratio by 0.2 points and wages by 0.42% in affected local labour markets
(Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020). In Germany, manufacturing job losses from robots were
fully offset by new service jobs, but the burden fell on young entrants rather than
incumbents (Dauth et al. 2021). The global stock of industrial robots reached 4.66 million
in 2024, growing 9% a year (IFR 2025).
2.5The labour share
The global corporate labour share fell about 5 points between 1975 and the early 2010s,
half of it explained by cheaper investment goods (Karabarbounis and Neiman 2014). The
ILO estimates that the global labour income share fell from 53.0% in 2014 to 52.4% in
2024, a 1.6-point fall since 2004. Estimates of the capital-labour elasticity of substitution
diverge: 1.25 for Karabarbounis and Neiman, 0.5-0.7 for US manufacturing in Oberfield and
Raval (2021). Polarisation of employment and wages in 1980-2005 is linked to the falling
cost of automating routine tasks (Autor and Dorn 2013). About 60% of US employment in
2018 was in job titles that did not exist in 1940 (Autor et al. 2024), which is the strongest
evidence that reinstatement is real but slow.
2.6 Distribution and aggregate demand
Whether a shift from wages to profits reduces demand depends on how much different
households spend. Micro estimates of the annual marginal propensity to consume lie
between 0.2 and 0.6, far above the values in representative-agent models; in simulated
wealth distributions it ranges from 0.48 in the bottom fifth to 0.06 in the top tenth (Carroll
et al. 2017). Average propensities to consume in the US fall from about 0.97 in the bottom
income quintile to about 0.48 in the top (Fisher et al. 2020). Globally, the top 10% receives
53% of pre-tax income and owns about 75% of wealth, while the bottom 50% receives 8%
and owns 2% (World Inequality Report 2026). Stock-flow consistent models (Godley and
Lavoie 2007) are the standard tool for tracing such distributional shifts through demand,
saving and debt.

Global public debt was just under 94% of GDP in 2025 and is projected to exceed 100% by
2029 (IMF Fiscal MonitorApril 2026), before anyAI shock. In the OECD, social
contributions (25.5%) and personal income taxes (23.7%) are the two largest sources of

2.8 Historical analogues
The closest precedent for a long transition gap is "Engels' pause" (Allen 2009). In Britain in
1800-1830 output per worker grew 0.63% a year while real wages did not grow at all; wages
caught up only after 1830 and grew faster than output only after 1860. A pause of three
decades is the historical benchmark against which a 2026-2046 transition should be
judged.
2.9Evidence on policy instruments
Unconditional transfers do not reduce work at the scale studied. Finland's 2017-2018
basic income experiment had small employment effects and clear improvements in
wellbeing, trust and economic security (Kela 2020). The Alaska Permanent Fund
Dividend had no effect on overall employment and raised part-time work (Jones and
Marinescu 2022). A universal basic income in Kenya increased enterprise formation
and did not reduce total hours worked (Banerjee et al. 2023).
Short-time work stabilises employment but does not reallocate it. Giupponi and
Landais (2023) find large effects on headcount and negative spillovers on employment
growth at other firms; German short-time work covered almost 6 million workers in
April 2020.
Sector-based training raises earnings substantially. Earnings gains of 11-40% persist
after training ends, mainly through placement in higher-wage sectors (Katz et al. 2022).
Collective agreements can govern AI use. The 2023 agreement of the Writers Guild of
America established that AI output is not literary material, that its use cannot be
imposed on writers, and that companies must disclose it.
2.10What remains open
No study yet measures AI's effect on tax receipts, on aggregate demand, or on social
stability. The size of the productivity gain is uncertain by an order of magnitude. Evidence
on reinstatement, the variable that matters most in this paper's results, comes almost
entirely from the twentieth century.

3.The original hypothesis paper and its critique
The hypothesis paper identifies the right mechanisms but leaves them unconnected
and unquantified, and omits three variables that change its conclusions: demographics,
timing, and where AI profits are booked. It asks to be tested rather than confirmed, and
this paper treats it that way.
The paper proposes a chain from automation to lower labour demand, weaker household
income and consumption, eventually lower profits, eroding labour-based tax bases, higher
welfare needs, and finally social instability and a shift toward automated security. It offers
three scenarios (inclusive growth, polarisation, structural disruption) and six indicators,
and explicitly asks for independent modelling, uncertainty and evidence.

4.1 Production and tasks
Following Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018, 2019), production combines a continuum of tasks
performed either by labour or by capital, here including AI. With a unit elasticity of
substitution across tasks, the labour share of income equals the labour task content of
production. AI adoption follows a logistic diffusion curve whose ceiling, timing and speed
are uncertain. Each yearAI displaces labour from a share of tasks equal to the exposed
share of employment, times the substitutable fraction of that exposure, times the
increase in adoption. After a lag, new tasks reinstate labour in proportion to past
displacement. Labour task content also erodes slowly for non-AI reasons, at a rate
calibrated to the historical decline of the labour share.

4.2 Labour market
The wage bill equals the labour share times output. Wages per worker follow trend
productivity net of the slow non-AI erosion of the labour share, pass through part of any
extra productivity growth, and respond to unemployment through a wage Phillips-curve
term whose slope is calibrated to post-1990 estimates (0.05 to 0.5). Labour demand is the
number of workers firms want at the current wage. Labour supply is the labour force from
UN projections. Unemployment is the gap between them, never below the structural rate;
when demand for labour exceeds supply, wages rise until the market clears. Displacement
therefore shows up first as unemployment and later as slower wage growth, as wages
adjust. Labour demand is derived from technology and output: the tasks left to labour
times output, valued at the wage of the no-AI world, adjusted for the wage gap with a
constant-output elasticity drawn between 0.15 and 0.75 (Hamermesh 1993). Lower wages
therefore restore only part of the jobs automation removes, and the wage bill falls with
employment.
Workers can also recover part of the AI-induced fall in the labour share through bargaining
or profit-sharing. This share is small under the status quo and is a policy lever.

4.3 Households
Each income group receives a fixed share of labour income, capital income in proportion
to its wealth, pensions, unemployment benefits, interest on public debt, and any policy
transfers. It consumes a group-specific share of disposable income and saves the rest.
Savings and retained corporate profits accumulate as wealth, so the distribution of capital
income evolves: if profits rise and the rich save more, wealth concentrates further.
4.4 Firms, investment and demand
Firms retain a share of after-tax profits. Investment responds to the profit share with an
uncertain elasticity, so higherAI profits can be recycled into capital spending (profit-led
demand) or not (wage-led demand). Output is the lower of supply and effective demand.
The no-AI world is assumed to run at potential, because monetary policy closes ordinary
demand gaps. A demand shortfall caused byAI, measured relative to that baseline,
reduces output with an uncertain pass-through that reflects how far monetary and fiscal
policy can offset it.
4.5 Government
Governments tax labour income, capital income and consumption, with rates calibrated to
each region's revenue and tax mix. They spend on purchases, pensions (rising with
ageing), unemployment benefits and interest. Debt evolves with the primary balance and
the gap between interest rates and growth.
4.6 Regions
A share of the AI-induced capital income generated in emerging and low-income
economies is booked in advanced economies and paid to their top 10%. This captures the
concentration of frontierAI firms and the stalled reallocation of taxing rights under OECD
Pillar One.
4.7 Accounting closure
In every region and year, the sectoral balances (household saving, firm saving net of
investment, government saving and net payments abroad) must sum to output minus
aggregate demand. The model checks this identity in every period of every simulation.
Across 4,000 paired runs the largest violation is 5 × 10⁻¹⁶ of GDP, machine precision: no
income leaks or appears from nowhere.

The model passes four external checks: it reproduces observed labour-share declines,

6.3Engels' pause
In Britain in 1800-1830 output per worker grew 0.63% a year while real wages stagnated
(Allen 2009). In the task framework this requires net displacement of about 0.6% a year
with negligible reinstatement, which would lower the labour share by 17% over 30 years.
This is within the range the model can generate: the lowest reinstatement values in the
prior, combined with fast adoption, produce net displacement of this size in advanced
economies. Modern US manufacturing displacement, about 1.1% a year in 1987-2017, was
larger still.
6.4 Macro magnitude
The model's median GDP gain after ten years (2036) is 1.3%, with a 5th-95th percentile
range of 0.3% to 3.5%. Acemoglu's (2025) upper bound of a 0.66% TFP gain over ten years
corresponds to roughly 0.9% of GDP, below the model's median of 1.3%. Considerably
larger estimates exist, but they assume broader and faster adoption than the evidence
through 2026 supports. The model therefore sits at the conservative end of the literature.
Larger productivity gains would soften the distributional result but not remove it. In the
fifth of simulations with the largest cost savings, the bottom half is still worse off in 2036
in 82% of runs (median −0.6%). When cost savings and gains on augmented tasks are
both in their top fifth, the bottom half's median loss shrinks to 0.3% and it is worse off in
64% of runs, while the top 10% gains 3.8%. By 2046 high-saving runs still leave the bottom
half worse off (median −2.1%). With larger gains, the bottom half's loss in 2036 becomes
small; the direction of inequality does not change.

plausibly on the data that exist. The forward-looking results remain conditional.

7. Results: aggregate paths
AI raises world output in every simulation and lowers the labour share in every
simulation; the median effect on output reaches 1.3% by 2036 and 3.2% by 2046, while
the labour share falls by 3.0 points by 2036 and 5.9 points by 2046. All results are
differences between paired runs of the same parameter draw with and without the AI
wave, so they isolate the effect ofAI from everything else in the baseline.
Three patterns stand out.
Output gains are modest and back-loaded. Diffusion takes time, and the productivity
gain is the cost saving on automated tasks, which is a fraction of their value. Even at
the 95th percentile, world GDP is 3.5% higher in 2036.

slightly as new tasks appear. In virtually no simulation (0.2%) does it return to the no￾AI path by 2046.

9. Global sensitivity analysis
What happens to households depends on when AI spreads and how fast new human
tasks appear; what happens to jobs depends mostly on how early and how broadlyAI
automates; neither depends much on how demand behaves. Total-order Sobol indices
measure the share of each outcome's variance that a parameter explains, alone or
through interactions. They were estimated from 23,552 paired runs using Saltelli sampling.
Four results matter for the argument.
1. Timing dominates the medium term. The year of fastest adoption explains 48% of the
variance of the bottom half's outcome in 2036, 60% of the GDP effect and 43% of
advanced-economy unemployment in 2036.
2. The long term depends on how the labour market absorbs displacement. By 2046
wage flexibility (25%), the number of new tasks per task displaced (19%) and the wage
elasticity of labour demand (19%) explain most of the variance of the bottom half's

10.1The mechanism:the base shifts,the rates do not
GDP rises with AI, but income moves from wages, taxed at an effective 28% in advanced
economies, to capital income, taxed at about 19%. Revenue falls even though the tax base
as a whole grows. Unemployment benefits during the transition add to the gap. The
shortfall is a matter of tax design, not of insufficient income.
10.2What would close the gap
In advanced economies AI shifts about 4.5 points of GDP from labour to capital income by
2036. Taxing that shift at an effective rate of about 41%, rather than the current 19%,
would keep the primary balance unchanged. That is somewhat above the rate at which
labour income is already taxed when social contributions are included (28%), because the
shift also raises unemployment benefits. Section 12 shows that a broader 5-point increase
in capital income taxation, recycled as transfers, improves the primary balance slightly
and lowers debt by 2046.
10.3Three caveats
Collection is the binding constraint. Capital is mobile and AI profits are concentrated
in a few firms and jurisdictions. The weakening of the global minimum tax in January
2026 and the stalled Pillar One make higher effective rates harder to achieve
unilaterally.
The fiscal effect is regressive across countries. Emerging and low-income economies
lose labour-tax revenue but collect less of the profit gain, because part of it is booked
abroad.
Tail risks are larger than medians. In the 5% worst simulations advanced-economy
debt is about 26 points higher by 2046, on top of a baseline already above 130%. In a
world where interest rates exceed growth, that matters more than its size suggests.
The hypothesis paper's concern about fiscal sustainability is therefore correct in
direction but mis-sized. AI is an aggravating factor on top of an ageing-driven debt
problem, not its main cause.
11. Social stability and automated policing
The model does not forecast unrest, and this paper does not attempt to: the evidence
links economic stress to unrest only through institutions, and no defensible
quantitative model of that link exists for 2026-2046. What the model does provide are
the inputs that the unrest literature associates with risk: a concentrated income loss for
the bottom half, a long unemployment peak in the late 2030s, and widening gaps between
regions.

creates a new axis of international grievance.
11.2 Automated policing
AI-assisted analysis (pattern search, triage, patrol planning) is already deployed and
cheap. Robotic patrol and autonomous coercion are costly, fragile and face liability gaps.
Police budgets are mostly wages, so automation saves money only if oversight and error
costs are ignored. The main risk is not a budget-driven substitution but an asymmetric
drift: surveillance capacity growing while social provision weakens, without any single
decision. That risk is highest where courts, the press and data-protection authorities are
weak.
Automated assistance with a named human decision-maker is governable. Autonomous
coercive decisions break existing rules on liability and due process and should be treated
as a legal bright line, not a fiscal option. The hypothesis paper's single "violence and social
instability" indicator should be split into peaceful protest, civil unrest, crime and political
violence, which have different causes and opposite policy implications.

13.The world in 2036: with and without AI
Under current policies, the world of 2036 with AI is not in crisis in this model: it is about
1% richer and measurably more unequal than it would be without AI, with markedly
higher unemployment in advanced economies, and the cost falls on the bottom half,
which loses a little on average, and far more heavily on the displaced workers within it,
whom the model does not separate. For the median person in the bottom half the
measured loss is small, about 1% of consumption in 2036, comparable to several months
of normal growth, and it more than triples by 2046. For displaced workers and young
entrants in exposed occupations it is large.

13.3 Verdictfor 2036
On the evidence and assumptions of this paper, AI does not produce a general crisis by
2036. It produces a redistribution and a long labour-market adjustment: a small gain for
the world as a whole, a large gain for owners of capital in advanced economies, a small
average loss for the bottom half, unemployment in advanced economies more than 4
points higher for several years and still higher in 2046, and a large loss for those whose
jobs are displaced before new work appears. Whether the bottom half feels better or
worse off depends on unmeasured benefits, such as cheaper services, which could offset
most of its measured loss in 2036 but not the larger loss of the 2040s. The concentration
of gains at the top holds in every simulation and the regional asymmetry in nearly all of
them; the size of the losses is uncertain.
This is a less dramatic picture than that of the illustrative model in the earlier version of
this work, which found a 6% loss for the median household in 2036. Section 14 explains
why.
14. Comparison with the earlier illustrative model
An earlier version of this work used a simple illustrative model that overstated both the
output gains and the household losses from AI by a factor of five to ten; its text, in turn,
initially understated the harm its own numbers showed. Both problems were found
during review and are documented here, because they illustrate how easily such analyses
mislead in either direction.

14.2Bias audit ofthe earlier report
Because this work was produced with an AI system made by an AI company, the earlier
report was audited for pro-AI bias at the author's request. Six problems were found and

1. A catch-up result driven by an assumption. The claim that households would catch up
with the no-AI world by 2046 depended on assuming that labour demand outgrows

worse off in 2046.

the basis of a parameter chosen by the author. This paper replaces that argument with
Sobol indices over a literature-based range (section 9).

were made explicit and tested. That is the main argument for the method used here.
14.4 Revision of October 2026: wage rigidity and labour demand
A review of the first edition of this paper found that its unemployment results rested on a
wage-response range that excluded the values found in recent evidence, and on a labour
demand that by construction followed labour supply. The range has been widened, a
related error corrected and labour demand rebuilt. Both changes are in replication
package version 2 ( aimodel_replication_v2.zip ).
1. Wage Phillips slope. The first edition drew the response of wages to unemployment
from 0.2 to 0.8, labelled as an assumption. US estimates fall from about 0.45 in 1991-
2008 to 0.14, not significantly different from zero, in 2009-2015 (Federal Reserve Bank
of San Francisco 2017), and the slope flattened after 2008 across ten advanced
economies (Bank of Canada 2019). The range is now 0.05 to 0.5.
2. Wage-trend error. In the first edition wages grew with productivity while the labour
share kept eroding for non-AI reasons, so even without AI the wage outgrew the wage
bill and unemployment drifted up (to about 6% worldwide by 2046, and to 7.8% in
advanced economies with very rigid wages). The wage trend now follows the non-AI
erosion, so the no-AI world stays at structural unemployment for any wage response,
as the historical decline of the labour share did. With the first edition's ranges this
correction moves no median by more than 0.2 points; it removes the small negative
unemployment effects after 2040 reported there, which came from comparing with a
drifting baseline.

whole horizon in most simulations. The direction of every distributional result is
unchanged; their size is larger, and the loss of the bottom half grows over time instead of

15. Limitations and research agenda
The results are conditional on a model that simplifies in ways that mostly understate
inequality within groups and exclude extreme capability scenarios. Readers should
treat the magnitudes as indicative and the directions as more robust.
15.1 Limitations
1. Unit elasticity across tasks. The labour share equals labour task content when wages
follow their no-AI path. With an elasticity above one, as Karabarbounis and Neiman
estimate, automation would lower the labour share more; below one, as Oberfield and
Raval find for manufacturing, less. Labour supply is exogenous (UN demographic
projections) and does not respond to AI through longer education, lower participation
or migration, and there is no skill, age or geographic mismatch between displaced
workers and new tasks. Without mismatch the model may understate how long
unemployment lasts; without a participation response, part of what it shows as
unemployment would in reality be hidden as inactivity.
2. No distribution within groups. Displaced workers and young entrants lose much more
than their group's average. The model reports group averages, so it understates
individual hardship.
3. No explicit age structure. The strongest current signal, the fall in hiring of young
workers, enters only through the evidence, not the model.
4. Exogenous adoption. AI spreads along an assumed curve; it does not respond to
wages, profits or regulation.
5. No inflation, exchange rates or trade. Interest rates are exogenous; demand shortfalls
are partly offset by an assumed policy response; regions are linked only through AI
profits.
6. Coarse regions. Three regions average very different countries, for example China and
India within the emerging group.
7. No extreme scenarios. The priors exclude transformative AI that automates most
cognitive and physical tasks within the horizon (Korinek and Suh 2024). Exposure is
allowed to grow by at most 60%. Results do not apply to such a world.
8. Uniform priors. They express plausible ranges, not likelihoods. Probability statements
are conditional on them.
9. Unmeasured effects. Welfare from cheaper services, health gains, energy costs and
changes in job quality are outside the model or reported separately.
10. Calibration gaps. Several regional values, listed in Appendix B, are assumptions within
published ranges rather than data.

15.2 Research agenda
Reinstatement. The rate at which new human tasks appear is the single most
important unknown for 2046 outcomes and the least measured. New occupational

Tax receipts. No study yet measures AI's effect on the composition of tax revenue.

Within-group distribution. Coupling the model with household microdata would show
how losses are distributed among displaced and non-displaced workers.

16. Conclusions
AI, as currently understood, is more likely to produce a gradual and regressive
redistribution than an economic collapse; the redistribution is robust, its size is
uncertain, and policy can reverse its sign for the bottom half at modest fiscal cost.
The hypothesis paper asked whether value created with less human labour would
circulate back to households and governments. Our answer is: only partly, unequally and
slowly. Output rises in every simulation. The labour share falls in every simulation. The top
10% gains in every simulation, mostly in advanced economies. The bottom half loses in
92% of simulations by 2036, by a median 1.0% of consumption, and by 3.3% in 2046.
Unemployment in advanced economies rises by a median 4.3 points by 2036 and is still 1.4
points higher in 2046. Emerging economies bear displacement without capturing the
profits, and low-income economies face their largest losses after 2036, as adoption
reaches them.
The hypothesis paper's most dramatic mechanism, a demand shortfall that eventually
undermines profits, does not appear in any plausible part of the parameter space. Its
fiscal concern is correct in direction but secondary to ageing. Its concern for social
stability is legitimate but cannot be quantified responsibly; what the model shows is a
concentrated, visible loss arriving when fiscal buffers are thinnest.
Three findings should guide policy:
1. Ownership matters more than speed, but it is not enough alone. Sharing AI-generated
capital income broadly, through an AI dividend fund, is the strongest single measure
for incomes and costs the budget almost nothing, but it protects the bottom half in
90% of simulations and fades as unemployment persists. Only a package that adds
income support, retraining and profit-sharing protects it in virtually every simulation.
2. Timing, the extent of automation and the labour market decide the outcome. When
and how broadlyAI spreads explains most of the variance in unemployment; how rigid
wages are, how fast new tasks appear and how far lower wages restore labour demand

C.4What a reader can change
The most useful experiments require editing a single line. Narrowing or widening a prior in
data.py shows how much a result depends on that assumption. Adding an entry to the
policy list in policy.py evaluates a new intervention against the same draws. Setting the
AI parameter to zero reproduces the no-AI world on its own. Readers who disagree with a
calibration choice are encouraged to change it and report the result.
